# Model overview

## Purpose

The Infrastructure Model is intended to identify and monitor stress across infrastructure systems and the people and institutions needed to sustain them. Its unit of analysis is primarily the U.S. state, with a national view for aggregation and comparison.

## Analytical dimensions

The model may organize indicators under these broad dimensions:

- Asset condition, age, capacity, and deferred maintenance.
- Capital investment, operating expenditure, and replacement requirements.
- Workforce size, age profile, retirements, recruitment, and training capacity.
- Population, household formation, migration, and regional demand.
- Material and supply-chain constraints that affect construction and repair.

Each indicator needs a documented definition, unit, geography, time period, source, and transformation method before it is used in a composite score.

## Workforce categories

Maintain separate measures for:

- **Primary infrastructure workforce:** direct construction, operations, inspection, maintenance, and repair of infrastructure assets and essential control systems.
- **Adjacent workforce:** infrastructure-dependent services and occupations. This is analytically important but is not part of the primary asset-workforce count.
- **Supporting workforce:** upstream manufacturing, materials, logistics, and other enabling suppliers. Report separately.

Avoid double counting workers who appear in multiple datasets or occupational categories.

## Stress indicator

The existing concept uses a 0–100 scale, where lower values indicate less measured stress and higher values indicate greater stress. Before implementation, document:

- Which indicators enter each domain and the overall score.
- Normalization and weighting methods.
- Missing-data treatment and confidence levels.
- Threshold definitions and their empirical or expert basis.
- Whether scores are comparable across states and years.

A composite score must always be traceable to its component indicators. Do not present the score as a probability or as a direct measure of imminent failure.

## Time horizons and scenarios

Long-range ideas such as workforce contractions, rural service changes, or a societal reset should be represented as explicit scenarios with assumptions, ranges, and sensitivity analysis. They are hypotheses to test, not established outcomes.

## Validation requirements

- Back-test against historical observations where possible.
- Compare modeled indicators with independent datasets.
- Test sensitivity to weights, assumptions, and missing values.
- Record model versions and changes to definitions.
- Publish limitations alongside charts and dashboards.
